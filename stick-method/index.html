The STICK Method: How to Make a Habit Survive Your Phone

Your feed has a plan for your evening. Here’s a better one, in five steps backed by research.

Dear Brain6 min read · Oct 31, 2026

It’s 1:12 AM. You meant to read tonight. Or stretch. Or write three lines in that journal you bought in September. Instead you picked up your phone “for one thing,” and an hour quietly disappeared.

If that sounds familiar, I want to say something first: this is not a willpower problem.

Your feed runs on one of the oldest loops in psychology. A cue (the buzz), a routine (the scroll), and a reward you can’t predict (the next post might be the good one). Unpredictable rewards build the stickiest habits we know of. It’s the same reason slot machines don’t pay out on a schedule.

So here’s the idea behind this piece. Instead of fighting a machine with motivation, we give our own habit the same structure the feed has: a clear cue, an easy routine, and a reward we can see.

I call it STICK. Five steps, each one built on research you can check. One of them involves a single sentence that took people from 35% follow-through to 91%. Another is the step almost everyone skips, and it’s the one that matters most.

Step 1

S — Select one tiny habit

Not five habits. One. And smaller than feels impressive.

Stanford researcher BJ Fogg describes behaviour as three things meeting at the same moment: motivation, ability, and a prompt.1 Motivation is the unreliable one. It’s high on Sunday night and gone by Wednesday. Ability is the one you control. Make the habit so small that it still happens on your worst day.

The research backs this up. When psychologists at University College London followed people building a new daily habit, simple ones like drinking a glass of water became automatic faster than demanding ones like doing 50 sit-ups.2

Try this

Pick the one habit that would change your winter most. Then shrink it until it takes under 20 minutes. “Read 10 pages,” not “read more.”

Step 2

T — Time-lock it

“I’ll do it later” is how the feed wins. Later has no cue. Your notifications do.

In 2002, researchers asked a group of people to exercise more. One group just got information about why exercise matters. Another got the same information plus one extra instruction: write down exactly when and where you’ll exercise next week.

91%

of the people who wrote that sentence exercised. In the other groups, it was 35 to 38%.3

Psychologists call these “if–then” plans implementation intentions, and a review of 94 studies found they have a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals.4 They work because so much of life already runs on context. One diary study found that about 43% of what people do each day is repeated in the same place, usually while thinking about something else.5 A fixed time and place becomes the cue that starts the habit for you.

There’s a second half to this step. Researchers found that just having your smartphone on the desk, face down and silent, reduces how much attention you have to work with.6 So the window doesn’t just need scheduling. It needs guarding.

Try this

Write it out: “When it’s [time] at [place], I will [habit].” Tie it to something that already happens, like after your morning coffee. For that window, put the phone in another room, or lock the apps that pull you away.

Step 3

I — Itemize it into a to-do list

“Journal” is foggy. “Open the notebook, write the date, write three lines about yesterday” is something your brain can actually start.

Unfinished goals have a way of nagging at you. A 2011 study found that simply making a specific plan for a goal quiets those intrusive thoughts and frees up your attention, even before you’ve done anything.7

And there’s a bonus. When Harvard researchers studied nearly 12,000 diary entries from people at work, the thing that lifted their mood and motivation most was making progress, even small progress.8 Every box you tick is a small win your brain notices.

Try this

Break the habit into three or four small steps, each with a few minutes on the clock. Make the first one physical and almost silly: “put on shoes,” “open the book.”

“The hardest part of any habit is the first thirty seconds. So make them easy.”

Step 4

C — Capture proof

Here’s the step almost everyone skips.

Most habit plans run on the honour system, and your brain is a very generous judge. “I basically did it” slowly turns into “I didn’t do it,” and you don’t notice the change until the habit is gone.

A review of 138 studies looked at what happens when people monitor their progress toward a goal. Monitoring helped. But it helped more when the progress was physically recorded, or shown to someone else.9 Evidence beats memory.

There’s a related idea in behavioural economics called a commitment device: a rule you set today that future-you can’t easily wriggle out of. In one well-known study, students who set binding deadlines for themselves did better than students with total freedom.10 A proof rule is a commitment device. You decide in advance what counts as done.

Try this

Decide now what “done” looks like. The open book. The running shoes, muddy. The clean kitchen. Take a photo every time. No photo, not done.

Step 5

K — Keep the record

This last step has two small parts: write one line about what you did, then mark the day on a calendar.

The writing matters more than it sounds. In a study at a large call centre, new employees who spent the last 15 minutes of their day writing about what they’d learned performed 23% better on a final test than those who didn’t.11 One line turns “something I did” into “something I learned about myself.”

The calendar matters too. Researchers studying coffee-shop loyalty cards found that people bought coffee faster the closer they got to a free one.12 It’s called the goal-gradient effect, and a growing chain of marked days does the same thing to you.

And the kindest finding of all: in the UCL habit study, missing a single day didn’t undo anyone’s progress.2 One slip is fine. The rule that protects you is simple.

Never miss twice.

Try this

Every time you finish, write one line: what you did, how it felt, one thing for tomorrow. Then mark the day somewhere you’ll see it.

Why winter is the right time

In that same UCL study, it took people a median of 66 days for a new habit to feel automatic.2 That can sound discouraging until you see the shape of it: the biggest gains come in the first few weeks. Every early repetition does more work than a later one.

Winter is when long, dark evenings pull all of us toward the couch and the feed. Which is exactly why it’s the best time to start. Build the base now, through November, and you walk into the new year with a habit that’s already running instead of a resolution that hasn’t started.

So, quickly, one more time:

  1. Select one tiny habit.
  2. Time-lock it to a when and a where.
  3. Itemize it into a short to-do list.
  4. Capture proof that it’s done.
  5. Keep the record: one line, one mark.

You can do all of this with a notebook, an alarm, a timer, your camera roll and a paper calendar. Honestly, that works.
