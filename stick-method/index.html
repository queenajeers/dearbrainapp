The STICK Method: 5 Simple Steps to Make a Good Habit Stick

Your phone is very good at grabbing your time. Here’s how to grab it back.

Dear Brain4 min read · Oct 31, 2026

It’s late at night. You wanted to read your book. But you picked up your phone “just for a minute.” Now an hour is gone.

This happens to everyone. And it’s not because you’re lazy.

Your phone is built to grab you. Each time you scroll, you might see something fun. Or you might not. Not knowing is what keeps you scrolling. A slot machine uses the same trick.

So here’s the plan. We won’t fight the phone with willpower. We’ll borrow its tricks and use them for a good habit.

We call it STICK. Five steps. Each one is backed by science. One step is a single sentence that helped almost three times more people stick to exercise. Another is the step most people skip.

Step 1

S — Select one small habit

Don’t start five habits. Start one. And make it small. So small it feels a bit too easy.

Why? Because feeling like doing something goes up and down. Some days you feel great. Some days you don’t. A tiny habit still happens on a bad day.1

Scientists in London watched people build new habits. Easy ones, like drinking a glass of water, turned automatic faster than hard ones, like doing 50 sit-ups.2

Try this

Pick one habit. Make it small. “Read 10 pages,” not “read more.”

Step 2

T — Time it

“I’ll do it later” almost never happens. Later isn’t a time.

Scientists once asked a group of people to exercise more. Some of them also wrote one sentence: when and where they would exercise.

91%

of the people who wrote that sentence exercised. Of everyone else, only about 1 in 3 did.3

That’s the power of a plan with a time and a place. Scientists have tested this idea in almost 100 studies, and it works again and again.4

Here’s why. Lots of what we do each day happens at the same time and place, almost on autopilot.5 Give your habit a set time, and the time itself reminds you.

One more thing. Just having your phone on the table makes it harder to think, even when it’s face down.6 So during your habit time, put it away.

Try this

Fill in the blanks: “At [time], in [place], I will [habit].” Then put your phone in another room for that time.

Step 3

I — Itemize it into a tiny to-do list

“Write in my journal” is fuzzy. Your brain doesn’t know where to start.

“Open the notebook. Write the date. Write three lines.” Now your brain knows exactly what to do.

When a goal is unfinished, your mind keeps poking you about it. Scientists found that making a clear plan makes the poking stop.7

And ticking a box feels good. Researchers read about 12,000 diary entries from people at work. What made them happiest? Making progress, even a little bit.8

Try this

Break your habit into 3 or 4 small steps. Make the first one super easy, like “put on your shoes.”

“The hardest part is starting. So make the start easy.”

Step 4

C — Capture proof

This is the step most people skip.

Our brains are too nice to us. We say, “I kind of did it.” Then one day we stop, and we don’t even notice.

Scientists looked at 138 studies. Keeping track of your progress helped people reach their goals. It helped even more when people wrote it down or showed it to someone.9

People also do better when they set a rule for themselves ahead of time.10 So decide today what your proof will be. Then you can’t trick yourself tomorrow.

Try this

Choose your proof photo. The open book. The muddy shoes. The clean kitchen. No photo? Not done.

Step 5

K — Keep a record

Two small things. Write one line about what you did. Then mark the day on a calendar.

Writing one line helps more than you’d think. New workers who spent 15 minutes at the end of each day writing about what they learned did 23% better on a test.11

Marking the calendar helps too. Think of a coffee card: buy 10, get one free. People bought coffee faster as they got closer to the free one.12 Your calendar works the same way. The more days you mark, the more you want to keep going.

And here’s the good news. Missing one day doesn’t ruin a habit.2 Just follow one rule:

Never miss two days in a row.

Try this

Write one line: What did I do? How did it feel? Then mark today.

Why winter is the perfect time

Habits take time. In the London study, it took about 66 days for a new habit to feel automatic.2 But the first few weeks count the most. That’s when a habit grows fastest.

Winter nights are long and dark. It’s easy to curl up with your phone. That’s exactly why winter is the best time to start. Begin now, and by the new year your habit will already be running.

Here are the five steps again:

  1. Select one small habit.
  2. Time it: pick a time and a place.
  3. Itemize it into a tiny to-do list.
  4. Capture a photo as proof.
  5. Keep a record: one line, one mark.

You can do all of this with a notebook, an alarm, a timer, your camera and a paper calendar. That works.

But that’s five things to juggle. So we put all five steps into one app: Dear Brain.

You pick a habit and give it a time. When that time comes, apps like TikTok and Instagram lock until you’re done. A timer walks you through each step. You snap a photo as proof. Then you write a line, and your calendar fills up.
