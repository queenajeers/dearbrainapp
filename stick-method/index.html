The STICK Method: five science-backed steps to make one habit stick this winter, in an age built to break it.

⏱ 4 min read🧠 12 studies✅ 5 steps

1:12 AM

You meant to read. Or run. Or journal. You picked up your phone “for one thing,” and an hour is gone. That’s not a willpower problem. It’s a design problem.

Feeds run on the oldest habit loop in psychology: a cue (the buzz), a routine (the scroll) and an unpredictable reward (the next post might be great). Unpredictable rewards are the hardest habits to quit. Slot machines work the same way.

So instead of fighting a machine with motivation, give your habit the same structure the feed has. That’s the whole idea behind STICK.

In one study, a single sentence took people from 35% follow-through to 91%. You’ll write that sentence in Step 2. And Step 4 is the one almost everyone skips.

S
Step 1 of 5

Select one tiny habit

Not five. One. And smaller than feels impressive.

🔬 The science

Behaviour happens when motivation, ability and a prompt meet at the same moment (Fogg’s behaviour model). Motivation swings day to day. Ability you can control: make the habit so small it still happens on your worst day.

When researchers tracked people building a new daily habit, simple ones like drinking water became automatic faster than demanding ones like 50 sit-ups. (Lally et al., 2010)

✍️ Do this

  • Pick the one habit that would make the biggest difference this winter.
  • Shrink it until it takes 2 to 20 minutes. “Read 10 pages,” not “read more.”

Next: the sentence that nearly tripled follow-through.

T
Step 2 of 5

Time-lock it

“I’ll do it later” is how the feed wins. Later has no cue. Your notifications do.

🔬 The science

91%exercised when they wrote when and where, vs 35–38% who only got motivation or nothing. (Milne, Orbell & Sheeran, 2002)

These “if–then” plans, called implementation intentions, had a medium-to-large effect across 94 studies. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006) They work because about 43% of what we do each day is repeated in the same context, often on autopilot. (Wood et al., 2002) A fixed time becomes the cue.

Just having your phone in sight reduces available brainpower, even when it’s face down. (Ward et al., 2017) So the window needs guarding, not just scheduling.

✍️ Do this

When it’s [time] at [place], I will [habit].
  • Pick the same time every day, tied to something that already happens (after coffee, after work).
  • For that window, lock the apps that pull you away. Remove the competing cue.

Next: why a blank habit is harder to start than a list.

I
Step 3 of 5

Itemize it into a to-do list

“Journal” is foggy. “Open notebook, write the date, three lines about yesterday” is a plan your brain can start.

🔬 The science

Unfinished goals keep nagging at your mind. Making a specific plan for them quiets that noise and frees up attention, even before you act. (Masicampo & Baumeister, 2011)

And of everything that lifts people’s motivation during a day, the biggest is making progress, even small progress. (Amabile & Kramer, 2011) Every ticked box is a tiny win.

✍️ Do this

  • Break the habit into 3–4 steps, each with a few minutes on the clock.
  • Make step one physical and silly-easy: “put on shoes,” “open the book.”

Next: the step almost everyone skips.

C
Step 4 of 5

Capture proof

Here it is. Most habit plans run on the honour system, and your brain is a generous judge. “Basically did it” slowly becomes “didn’t do it.”

🔬 The science

A review of 138 studies found that monitoring your progress raises your chances of reaching a goal, and the effect is stronger when progress is physically recorded or shown to someone. (Harkin et al., 2016)

Commitment devices work for the same reason: when present-you sets a rule future-you can’t wriggle out of, people follow through more. (Ariely & Wertenbroch, 2002)

✍️ Do this

  • Decide now what “done” looks like: the open book, the running shoes by the door, the clean kitchen.
  • Snap a photo of it every time. No photo, not done.

Next: turn today into a streak you won’t want to break.

K
Step 5 of 5

Keep the record

Two parts: write one line about it, then mark the day on a calendar.

🔬 The science

+23%better performance for trainees who spent 15 minutes at the end of the day writing what they learned. (Di Stefano et al., 2014)

A visible chain of marked days pulls you forward. People speed up as they see themselves getting closer to a goal, the goal-gradient effect. (Kivetz et al., 2006)

And the good news: missing one day did not undo habit formation in the research. (Lally et al., 2010) Missing two in a row is where it slips.

✍️ Do this

  • Write one line: what you did, how it felt, one thing for tomorrow.
  • Mark the day. Keep the calendar where you’ll see it.
  • Rule: never miss twice.

❄️ Why winter, why now

In the habit study, it took a median of 66 days for a habit to feel automatic. But the curve is steepest at the start: the early repetitions do the most work.

Long nights pull everyone toward the couch and the feed. That’s exactly why a Winter Arc works: build the base now, and you walk into the new year with a habit already running.
